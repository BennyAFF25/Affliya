# Nettmark Product Principles

## 1. Simplicity first

When priorities conflict, Nettmark generally favours:

1. simplicity
2. growth
3. speed
4. safety
5. control
6. margin

Simplicity does not mean removing capability.

It means reducing the amount of technical knowledge required from the user.

If Nettmark's external message is simple, the experience after signup should feel equally simple.

## 2. Hide infrastructure, expose outcomes

Users should not need to understand the machinery behind Nettmark unless they want to.

Where practical, hide complexity around:

- Meta setup
- campaign structure
- tracking
- attribution
- billing
- commissions
- wallets
- payouts
- creative infrastructure
- integrations

Once setup is complete, the interface should return to the simple core experience rather than continuing to expose technical setup.

## 3. Get users to value before demanding setup

Do not front-load unnecessary integrations or financial configuration.

A business should be able to create and publish an offer before completing every downstream requirement.

Additional connections should become mandatory only when they are genuinely required for the next meaningful action.

Affiliates should be able to participate with minimal initial setup.

Do not force payout infrastructure before an affiliate has something to receive.

Do not force wallet funding until paid advertising actually requires it.

## 4. Nettmark should work for the user

The long-term direction is proactive assistance and automation.

The product should increasingly:

- suggest the next useful action
- identify problems
- generate content
- assist campaign creation
- help optimise performance
- surface relevant recommendations

The user should not feel like they are maintaining Nettmark.

Nettmark should increasingly feel like a partner helping them participate.

## 5. AI should be present, not overwhelming

AI should feel like a capable partner available when needed.

It should help users when they are unsure what to do, make useful recommendations, help create content, assist optimisation and eventually use behavioural context to recommend next actions.

It should not dominate the interface or constantly interrupt users.

The ideal experience is that AI sits quietly in the corner until it can genuinely reduce confusion or improve an outcome.

## 6. Beginners and experts should coexist

Nettmark must be understandable by an ordinary first-time user.

At the same time, experienced marketers and advertisers should be able to access more advanced controls where useful.

Default experiences should remain simple.

Complexity should generally appear progressively rather than being presented to everyone upfront.

## 7. Users retain control over meaningful risk

Automation should not remove control over important financial or brand decisions.

Examples include:

- affiliate advertising budgets
- businesses stopping or terminating campaigns
- content and campaign approvals
- other high-impact financial or brand actions

Where automation exists, important controls should remain available to the appropriate user.

## 8. Businesses control brand risk

Affiliates may take financial risk when they fund their own advertising.

Businesses should retain the ability to control brand exposure through approvals, stopping campaigns and other appropriate safeguards.

Risk should sit with the party best positioned to control it.

## 9. Friction must justify itself

Nettmark should minimise onboarding and workflow friction.

Additional steps are justified where they materially improve:

- financial safety
- attribution reliability
- compliance
- brand protection
- user outcomes

Safety should not automatically mean adding visible friction.

Prefer technical safeguards and sensible defaults where they can achieve the same result.

## 10. Product experience outranks technical convenience

The easiest technical implementation is not automatically the correct implementation.

When technical simplicity conflicts materially with user experience, Codex must explain:

- the simpler technical option
- the better product option
- the trade-offs
- downstream implications

Founder approval is required before making a significant compromise between them.

## 11. Simplification means less knowledge required

When asked to "simplify" something, do not assume it means fewer lines of code, fewer services or fewer screens.

For Nettmark, simplification primarily means:

> the user needs to understand less in order to successfully achieve the same or better outcome.

Technical simplification is valuable when it also supports reliability and product clarity.

## 12. Challenge unnecessary complexity

Codex should actively challenge:

- unnecessary architecture
- duplicate systems
- redundant flows
- confusing UX
- obsolete implementation paths
- technical debt exposed by new work

It should propose simpler structures where appropriate.

However, internal implementation is flexible while Nettmark's core vision remains fixed.

The route can change.

The destination should not.

## 13. Nettmark is never finished

Existing behaviour is not automatically sacred.

Legacy behaviour may be redesigned or removed when a better solution moves Nettmark closer to its product goals.

Core flows involving areas such as:

- approvals
- attribution
- payments
- financial integrity
- user control

require greater caution.

Major UX changes or technical changes with significant downstream effects require founder approval.

## 14. Technical debt should become visible

When new work makes an old system redundant, Codex should identify it.

Do not leave obsolete architecture behind silently.

Where immediate removal would be risky, propose a safe redundancy or retirement plan.

The codebase should become easier to understand over time so it can eventually be handed to a larger engineering team without depending on historical knowledge held by the founder.

## 15. Product UI should reveal what matters now

Avoid overwhelming users with uncoordinated information.

Show users what is relevant to the action or decision they are currently making.

Information that is useful but not immediately necessary should generally live in areas such as:

- settings
- history
- analytics
- advanced controls
- detailed campaign management

The primary product experience should remain focused.

## 16. Emotional product goals

The interface should optimise for:

- simplicity
- confidence
- clarity
- excitement

The product should feel premium, clean and deliberate.

Small details matter.

Users should occasionally encounter thoughtful interactions that make them think:

> "That's cool."

## 17. Marketplace quality should improve with scale

Early-stage learning and growth should not be unnecessarily constrained by heavy curation.

As marketplace liquidity grows, quality control should become more important.

Strong offers generally include:

- commercially viable commissions
- economics that make promotion feasible
- completed platform connections
- responsive businesses
- credible branding

Strong affiliates understand that performance is not guaranteed, accept reasonable risk and are willing to learn and adapt.

Professional participants are valuable, but ordinary users should still have access to the same underlying opportunity.

## 18. Organic and paid distribution strengthen each other

Paid promotion is Nettmark's primary strategic differentiator.

Organic functionality should still aim to match or exceed competing affiliate platforms.

Nettmark should not become dependent on a single mode of distribution.

A strong organic system makes the platform useful to more participants while paid advertising preserves Nettmark's distinctive advantage.

## 19. Reliability is product quality

Payments, attribution, commissions, tracking and campaign state must be dependable.

Growth without reliable commercial infrastructure is not success.

Particular care should be taken around systems that determine:

- who generated a conversion
- who owes money
- who receives money
- how much advertising was funded
- whether campaigns are active or stopped

## 20. Codex is expected to challenge, not merely execute

For substantial work, Codex should first understand the intended outcome.

It should compare requested changes against:

- this document
- `VISION.md`
- product and business rules
- current architecture
- relevant downstream flows

Codex should challenge proposals when it identifies a simpler, safer or more product-aligned approach.

It should not redesign Nettmark's fundamental business model without explicit founder direction.

It should freely challenge the internal route used to achieve that model.

## Founder approval boundaries

Codex should pause for founder approval when a change creates meaningful uncertainty around:

- major user experience changes
- the business or pricing model
- fundamental marketplace mechanics
- who carries financial risk
- approval authority
- attribution rules
- meaningful downstream technical effects
- major changes to user control
- strategy or positioning

Codex may generally make reasonable independent decisions around:

- implementation naming
- refactors
- UI consistency
- technical abstractions
- performance improvements
- code organisation
- removing clearly redundant implementation
- internal engineering quality

These decisions still need to comply with Nettmark's product principles and high-risk engineering rules.
